# Skills, System, Rhythm, Value

**A framework for turning post-sale teams into growth engines |  and for diagnosing why the last enablement program didn't work.**

Every company with a recurring revenue model eventually asks the same question: how do we get the people who already talk to our customers to grow those customers?  Customer success teams, technical account managers, service delivery leads |  they own the relationships, they see the expansion signals first, and most of them were never taught to sell.

So the company buys sales training.  And ninety days later, nothing has moved.

This framework explains why, and what to do instead.  It comes from thirty years of watching enablement programs live and die |  in enterprise field service deals, in Microsoft's Business Value Engineering motion, and in mid-market SaaS expansion teams.  The short version: **behavior change fails in four predictable places, and a program only works when it patches all four.**

---

## The Four Failure Modes

Ask these four questions about any revenue team.  Whichever fails first is where the needle is stuck.

1. **Can they have the conversation?**  If not, the failure is skill.
2. **Does anything in their workflow trigger the conversation?**  If not, the failure is system.
3. **Does anyone inspect it weekly?**  If not, the failure is rhythm.
4. **Does the customer leave holding a number their CFO believes?**  If not, the failure is value.

Four failure modes, four layers.  Remove any one and you can name the exact way the program dies.

```
SKILLS  |  can they have the conversation?
SYSTEM  |  does the workflow trigger it?
RHYTHM  |  does management inspect it?
VALUE   |  does the customer leave holding a number their CFO believes?
```

Skills without System is a workshop everyone enjoyed and nobody applied.  System without Skills is a playbook nobody can run.  Both without Rhythm is a strong first month and a quiet second one.  All three without Value is a team having great conversations that never survive the customer's own approval chain.

The layers map cleanly to the behavioral science: COM-B calls them capability, opportunity, and motivation.  This framework adds the fourth because B2B is different |  the buying decision happens in a meeting you are not in, and your champion needs something to carry into that room.

## Layer 1 | Skills

**The failure it patches: they can't do it.**

A slide deck is not a skill.  A skill is formed thru attempts with feedback on real material |  Ericsson's deliberate practice research, the 70-20-10 model, and every sports coach in history agree on this.  The classroom exists to set up live reps, not the other way round.

Design rules:

- **Teach the smallest skill set that changes the conversation.**  For a post-sale team that is three skills: elicit a customer objective, qualify an opportunity against it, defend value under objection.  Not "become a salesperson."  That framing triggers identity resistance in people who chose customer success precisely because they didn't want to carry a bag.
- **Every session feeds a live rep.**  Between sessions, each participant runs the skill on a real account and brings the result back.  The program should generate live pipeline while it runs |  that is both the learning mechanism and the proof.
- **Respect the decay math.**  Neil Rackham's Huthwaite research |  still the largest empirical study of sales effectiveness ever run |  found that roughly 87% of trained sales skills are lost within a month without reinforcement.  Training alone is not a strategy.  It is a deposit that evaporates unless the next two layers hold it.

## Layer 2 | System

**The failure it patches: they can't do it in the flow of work.**

BJ Fogg's behavior model says Behavior = Motivation × Ability × Prompt.  Enablement programs obsess over motivation and ability and ignore the prompt |  the trigger in the workflow that fires the behavior at the right moment.  A CSM juggling forty accounts does not need to remember your discovery questions.  They need the question guide open on the second screen when the trigger fires.

Design rules:

- **Every asset attaches to a trigger that already exists.**  A contract review date notification.  A QBR on the calendar.  A usage report showing seat growth.  A support ticket pattern.  An asset without a trigger is shelfware, which is the standard fate of sales playbooks and the reason buyers are rightly cynical about them.
- **Ship artifacts, not advice.**  Discovery maps.  Expansion signal lists.  Churn early-warning lists.  Qualification templates.  Objection scripts.  Talk tracks.  Gawande's Checklist Manifesto logic applies: experts fail from inconsistent execution under load, not from ignorance.
- **Mine the product the team already uses.**  Most platforms contain dormant triggers |  renewal date fields, adoption dashboards, usage alerts |  that can become the prompt layer with zero product work.  Find them before building anything new.

### The Segmentation Sub-Layer

One system does not fit one team, because one team rarely covers one kind of customer.  Coverage ratio decides what motion is physically possible, and the system has to be built per segment or it will quietly demand the impossible:

| Segment | Typical coverage | The motion the system must support | What the assets look like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tech-touch / SMB | One person to hundreds of accounts | Signal-driven.  No discovery calls |  the trigger IS the touch | Signal library wired to templated outreach.  Trigger fires, play runs, done |
| Mid-market | One person to tens of accounts | Full consultative motion | Discovery maps, value cards, QBR templates |  the complete asset library |
| Enterprise / strategic | One person to a handful of accounts | Account planning | Multi-site expansion maps, stakeholder maps, multi-year value roadmaps |

The classic failure is writing one playbook for the mid-market motion and handing it to everyone.  The tech-touch team cannot run it |  at 500:1 there are no discovery calls, only signals |  so they ignore it, and the program gets blamed for a coverage-model mismatch.  Segment the system first: same Skills foundation, same Rhythm cadence, but a different trigger-and-asset kit per coverage tier.  A signal-driven play is not a lesser version of the consultative motion.  It is the correct motion for its ratio.

## Layer 3 | Rhythm

**The failure it patches: they stop doing it.**

Two sources carry this layer.  *Cracking the Sales Management Code* proved that you cannot manage a result, only an activity.  Net revenue retention is a result |  nobody can "do" NRR on a Tuesday.  "Two qualified expansion conversations this week" is an activity: manageable, inspectable, coachable.  *The 4 Disciplines of Execution* adds the mechanism: a cadence of accountability |  a short, unmissable, recurring meeting where each person reports on their lead measure.

Design rules:

- **The rhythm must be cheap enough to survive a busy calendar.**  Three pipeline stages.  One sheet.  Thirty minutes a week.  Any heavier and it gets skipped in week five, and skipping is fatal because rhythm is the decay-prevention layer.
- **Re-instrument the manager, not just the rep.**  Front-line manager behavior is the strongest predictor of whether training sticks.  If the Monday meeting still only asks about support tickets, the team is back to reactive in three weeks |  because the meeting IS the culture.  You are not training twenty people; you are re-instrumenting four Monday meetings.
- **Forecast accuracy is coached, not punished.**  The first quarter of forecasts will be wrong.  The point is that they exist, and that someone asks why.
- **Translate the board metric into a Tuesday number.**  Nobody can act on "get NRR to 110%."  Divide the expansion target by the team, then by the month: it usually lands somewhere like two qualified expansion conversations a week and two closes a month per person.  That is a rhythm a human can hold, and it is the number the weekly cadence inspects.  When the board metric moves two quarters later, everyone can trace exactly why.

## Layer 4 | Value

**The failure it patches: the deal dies in the buyer's building.**

The conversation happened.  The trigger fired.  The manager inspected it.  And the expansion still stalled |  because the champion walked into their CFO's office with enthusiasm instead of arithmetic.  B2B deals are not closed in your meeting.  They are closed in a meeting you are not in, and Quantifiable Business Value is what your champion carries into that room.

This is the Business Value Engineering discipline that enterprise vendors staff whole teams for, miniaturized so a mid-market team can run it without a BVE department.

**Every value claim reduces to one equation:**

```
Baseline metric  ×  improvement  ×  unit economics  =  value
```

A feature name is not a business case.  "Your twenty engineers average 3.1 jobs a day; a 20% scheduling gain is roughly 0.6 jobs per engineer per day; at your average job value that is six figures of annual capacity" |  that is a business case.  Always present three cases: conservative, expected, best.  The conservative case is the one that survives the CFO.

**The number must be co-authored.**  A vendor's number is marketing.  The customer's number is truth.  The discovery skill from Layer 1 exists to extract the baseline from the customer |  "how many jobs does an engineer do a day?  what is an average job worth?" |  and then do the multiplication in front of them.  When they ask where the number came from, the answer is: from you.  Customer-authored math is the only math that survives procurement.

**The incumbent's advantage: baselines are queries, not questions.**  When the seller is the vendor the customer already runs on, the discovery baselines are sitting in the product.  Jobs per tech per day, cycle times, compliance coverage, adoption trends |  it is all telemetry.  The motion flips from interrogation to evidence: not "how many jobs does an engineer do a day?" but "your engineers averaged 2.8 jobs a day last quarter and travel ate 31% of their time |  what would 3.4 mean for the business?"  Nobody feels pitched by their own numbers, which is why this move dissolves the salesy-vendor objection better than any talk track.  The System asset that carries it is an **Account Value Snapshot** |  a handful of metrics on one page, pulled from the product before every strategic conversation, mandatory prep for any QBR.  And if the data turns out to be hard for the team to reach, that is not a blocker.  It is a finding to hand the product and BI teams, and usually a wider engagement.

**Value drivers vary by vertical, so ship value cards, not one calculator.**  The four universal value categories are grow revenue, cut cost, avoid risk, and free up cash |  but every industry weights them differently and speaks its own dialect.  Three worked examples from field service:

| Vertical | What value means to them | Drivers that quantify | The language rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trade contractor (HVAC, M&E, electrical) | Margin and cash | Jobs per engineer per day, first-time fix, travel time, quote-to-cash days, margin per job | Talk profit.  Owner-operated businesses feel every pound |
| Facilities management provider | Contract survival | SLA penalty avoidance, contract renewal rate, subcontractor cost leakage, compliance evidence | Talk contracts.  Their revenue IS retention |  they resell your reliability to their client |
| Public sector estate (health, education, government) | Duty and defensibility | Statutory compliance, audit readiness, regulator exposure, maintenance backlog, budget certainty | Never lead with ROI.  Lead with risk, duty, and audit.  "Defensible in front of the auditor" outsells "18% savings" every time |

A seller who pitches a public-sector estates director on margin per job has already lost.  Same product, different value card.

**Where Value plugs into the other layers:** Skills gains a quantification block |  extracting baselines in discovery and doing conservative math live.  System gains the value cards and a one-page calculator filled in during the call.  Rhythm gains the single inspection question that makes forecasts stop being fiction: **"What's the number, and who authored it?"**  No customer-validated value hypothesis, no advance in the pipeline.

## Running the Framework

**As a diagnostic.**  Four questions in a first meeting tell you where any client's needle is stuck.  Whichever layer fails first is where the engagement starts.  Do not sell a boot camp to a team whose failure mode is rhythm.

**As a program architecture.**  The layers tier naturally: Skills is the training engagement, System is the asset library, Rhythm is the operating cadence, Value is the content that flows thru all three.  The tiering is not packaging spin |  it is the theory itself, which makes the upsell honest: you can buy Skills alone, and the decay research tells you what happens next.

**As a measurement plan.**  Each layer produces its own evidence.  Skills shows up in role plays and live calls.  System shows up in asset usage and trigger coverage.  Rhythm shows up in cadence held and forecasts submitted.  Value shows up in customer-authored business cases.  The result |  pipeline, then revenue, then retention |  follows in that order, on roughly a 30/60/90 arc.

**The one-line version:**

> Training changes what people can do.  Systems change what they actually do.  Rhythm changes what they still do in ninety days.  Value changes what the customer's CFO approves.  The needle only moves when all four hold.
