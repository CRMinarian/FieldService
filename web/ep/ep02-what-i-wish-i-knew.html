POINT 1 [PILL: 1 · Document your processes first]

The very first thing I wish someone had told me is this. Document your processes before you ever touch the software.

Back in the day we did what we called ride alongs, or envisioning workshops. We'd interview people, map out how they actually work, and help them define what the future should look like. And that process was never just documentation. It was about building consensus, and understanding how people felt about their jobs.

Here's what's changed. You can now do a much more powerful version of this with AI.

If you have existing standard operating procedures, upload them to SharePoint or Teams, and have an agent compare them directly to Microsoft's official Business Process Catalog for Field Service. You get a gap fit analysis in hours instead of weeks.

And even if you have no documentation at all, run a few workshops. Record the meetings. Transcribe them. Drop the transcripts into SharePoint, and literally ask AI to create standard operating procedures from those conversations, and tell you the sentiment in the room.

So the same exercise is now both a technical exercise and a team building exercise. That's new. Use it.

POINT 2 [PILL: 2 · Data quality is everything]

Second thing I wish I knew. Data quality is everything.

If you want to measure whether your implementation actually worked, you need good KPIs. First Time Fix Rate. Mean Time Between Failures. Utilization. But KPIs are worthless without clean, consistent data underneath them.

The biggest mistake I see is companies just not taking data seriously. You can waste ten percent of your technicians' time on bad data and bad process alone. Ten percent.

And here's the key. Don't start by picking KPIs. Start by deciding what business outcomes you actually want. Then work backwards to the KPIs that tell you whether you're getting them.

Because once you start measuring something, people will manage to it. Your technicians are smart. They will game whatever you measure. So report the positive things you want more of, and one or two negative things you want stopped. Returns. Complaints. Low CSAT.

The good news is Dynamics has a semantic data layer built in. Shape your data correctly from day one and you get real insights automatically, even when some of it comes from the ERP.

Bottom line. Good data isn't nice to have. It's the foundation of whether the whole thing succeeds or fails.

POINT 3 [PILL: 3 · Change management is not optional]

The hardest part of any Field Service implementation isn't the technology. It's change management.

One of the biggest mistakes people make is treating Field Service like an ERP project. With ERP you can do a big bang cutover over a weekend. Field Service does not work that way. You're running a live service business every single day while you're trying to change it.

And too often the project is driven by IT and senior leadership, with almost no actual service people in the room making decisions. Then they build this beautiful new system, roll it out to the technicians and dispatchers all at once, and act surprised when it goes sideways.

My strong recommendation is a pod approach. Start small. One pilot team. Give them the new tools and processes and let them prove it works. When that pod starts outperforming the old way, the rest of the organization sees it with their own eyes. Nobody argues with a scoreboard.

You can also work bottom up. Find the old painful processes nobody likes and fix those first. Or start with the maintenance heavy work. Your new techs don't want those jobs anyway, and your experienced people can optimize them beautifully.

Real change happens in small, measured steps with visible wins. Not in one giant rip and replace.

POINT 4 [PILL: 4 · Don't turn on RSO too early]

Number four is one of the most expensive mistakes I see. Turning on Resource Scheduling Optimization way too early.

Here's the truth. Scheduling is incredibly complex, and most people dramatically underestimate it. RSO is not a checkbox. It's a completely different way of doing scheduling.

Most dispatchers are rightfully skeptical of it. It threatens their expertise. And the system is non deterministic, meaning it can give you different results every time you run it. That is genuinely hard for people to trust.

The biggest mistake is treating RSO like a magic button. You cannot just flip it on. If your data isn't clean, your processes aren't solid, and your team doesn't understand the economics of scheduling, RSO makes your problems worse. Faster, and with more confidence.

POINT 5 [PILL: 5 · Choose your partner wisely]

Finally, number five. Choose your implementation partner very carefully. And this matters more right now than it ever has.

Here's the reality in 2026. The partner channel is going through massive disruption because of AI. Discovery, documentation, data work. Things that took months now happen in days. That's putting real pressure on the big partners, especially the publicly traded ones.

And because of that, I'm seeing extremely high turnover on delivery teams. You cannot assume the people who start your project will be there when it ends.

So here's my advice. Don't lock yourself into one big long term contract with a single partner. Consider a tiered approach. Bring in one highly experienced person, an MVP or a senior consultant, to guide the strategy and act as your bridge. Then be willing to use different partners for different phases.

And most importantly, focus on who is actually doing the work, not the logo on the proposal. Ask hard questions about team continuity, knowledge handoff, and how they plan to keep the same people on your project from kickoff to go live.

Because right now, the power in the partner ecosystem is shifting back toward you, the customer. Use it.
